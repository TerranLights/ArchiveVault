## Findings
- Stability in Cascadia is shaped by a long history of environmental and cultural continuity. The Northwest Coast cultural area has "stable cultural patterns probably have existed for more than five thousand years," and the intermontane Northwest experienced "10,000 years of stable environmental conditions" with "cultural and social changes were modest until the historic period." This deep history of stability influences Cascadian cultural values around continuity, tradition, and place.

- The Pacific Northwest's environmental stability was disrupted by climate change, which the Fifth National Climate Assessment notes has "disrupted sense of place in the Northwest, affecting noneconomic values such as proximity and access to nature and residents' feelings of security and stability." This suggests that stability is understood as intertwined with a sense of place and heritage.

- Cascadian values emphasize community resilience and self-sufficiency as mechanisms for maintaining stability. The Core Principles of the Cascadia Department of Bioregion include "Community Resilience and Empowerment" with "self reliance, sustainability, networking and locally based disaster preparedness." The movement emphasizes "connections between the individual, the community and the bioregion which increase the ability of each to support itself."

- The Cascadia Movement emphasizes "local autonomy, to empower individuals and communities to better represent their own needs, and to create sustainable local economies through bioregional planning." It "encourages people to think bioregionally, and therefore locally; to build community resilience; and to create new pathways for communication, politics, and interdependence that better reflect the social, cultural, and political realities of our region."

- Stability is also maintained through local governance and democratic institutions. The Core Principles include "Dynamic and Open Governance" with "local democracy, decentralizing existing centralized power structures, using delegates rather than representatives." The movement supports "policies that bring our government into the 21st century that are accountable and transparent, and allow for every person to substantially participate in the issues that most affect their lives."

- Back-to-land movements in the Pacific Northwest reflect a cultural value on stability and continuity. The WSU Magazine article "Defining Utopia" describes how "people have been creating small countercultural communities centered around the back-to-land movement since the industrial revolution" and that "the Pacific Northwest has been fertile ground for counterculture groups over the last 100 years." These communities "resisted industrialism as a way to resist industrial capitalism and turn toward anarchism, socialism, and communism" in the 1890s, and "in the 1970s in the wake of the environmental movement, as radical counterculturalists reached back to their small-scale farming roots in order to become more sustainable and extend the longevity of the planet."

- Bioregional regeneration in Whatcom County and Cascadia reflects a value on stability through "community-led watershed governance, regenerative agricultural practices, and permaculture principles" to "create self-healing landscapes and resilient local economies." The movement aims to "establish a local steady state economy" and provides "a countervailing cultural and civic space."

- The Cascadia Department of Bioregion emphasizes "durable place-based organizing" and "stewards the shared infrastructure the movement relies on, including this website, the Cascadia Directory, and the flag." It supports "locally led teams in other bioregions to model, template, and launch Departments of Bioregion in their own watersheds" to "make sure durable place-based organizing can take root elsewhere."

- Stability in Cascadia is also understood as resilience in the face of disruption. The bioregional movement emphasizes "self-healing landscapes and resilient local economies" and "local steady state economy" as alternatives to growth-oriented models. This suggests that stability is valued not as stasis but as a resilient, adaptable continuity.

- Disruption of stability in Cascadia would be perceived as threats to community, environment, and place. The Fifth National Climate Assessment notes that "climate change poses an existential threat to the ability of Northwest communities to maintain their sense of place and heritage for future generations." This suggests that actions that undermine community continuity or environmental stability would be seen as threatening to Cascadian values.
