## Findings
- Stubborn persistence in Cascadia is expressed primarily through environmental activism and bioregional protection. The "Thin Green Line" movement demonstrates this: environmentalists, Indian tribes, ranchers, politicians, doctors, fishermen, and even windsurfers worked for a decade to fend off more than 20 proposals to ship fossil fuels across the Pacific, from near Prince Rupert, British Columbia, clear south to San Luis Obispo, California. "Against the odds, and even their own expectations, activists blocked nearly every effort to use the region's ports to expand the global fossil fuel trade between 2004 and 2017."

- The persistence of Cascadian environmental activists was characterized by "relentless tenacity," as described in the Cascadia PBS article. "We built a coordinated regional strategy that we deployed with relentless tenacity," said Beth Doglio, co-director of Climate Solutions. This reflects a cultural value on determined, persistent action in defense of community and environmental interests.

- The Sightline Institute describes the region's opposition movement as having "fought these projects over and over again, and defeated even the best-capitalized and politically-connected projects." This suggests that stubbornness against powerful external interests is valued and respected in Cascadian culture.

- The Republic of Cascadia: A Brief Timeline of Independence provides historical context for Cascadian stubbornness. "The citizens of Oregon, many who felt it was through their efforts that the territory had been acquired, were outraged at what they felt was then a refusal by the US government to grant them necessary assistance and protection." This reflects a cultural value on self-reliance and local decision-making. "In addition, the inability for citizens to elect their own representatives, those instead being appointed by the Eastern federal government, led to a dramatic increase of dissatisfaction throughout the 1850's, culminating in a growing movement to demand Independence."

- The "Western spirit" described in the Oregon Historical Quarterly provides further historical grounding: "The fur traders who blazed the trail to the West, and the ranchers and farmers who followed in their wake forging the broader path for civilization were not aristocrats, but the common people – rugged, self-reliant and ambitious… seeking cheap lands, and a chance to work out their political and social ideas free from the aristocratic organization of the East." "The early isolation of the West, and the completeness of its geographic separation from the political center of the nation fostered an intense feeling of local independence."

- The State of Jefferson movement (1940-1941) reflects a "firewall" tradition: "During 1940 and 1941, organizers attracted massive media attention by arming themselves and blockading Highway 99 to the south of Yreka where they collected tolls from motorists and passed out proclamations of independence." In 1956, "groups from Cave Junction, Oregon and Dunsmuir, California threatened to tear Southern Oregon and Northern California from their respective state rulers to form the State of Shasta." These reflect a persistent tradition of resisting outside authority.

- The CascadiaNow article describes a "fiercely independent spirit" as one of the primary goals of the Cascadian Independence Project. "Not in the sense that we have a military, or rigidly defined borders that would be defended to the death. No, Cascadia is a nation in the sense that it is a gathering of individuals and communities who reflect similar desires and needs, a unique cultural identity, and, most importantly, a common future." This suggests that independence is valued but within a framework of community and shared identity.

- The Core Principles of the Cascadia Department of Bioregion state that "Cascadians have a shared affinity with those living here that stems from this place we live, a ruggedly independent spirit, and a belief in equality and equity." This reflects a cultural value on independent spirit combined with community.

- Stubbornness that is directed toward environmental protection and community autonomy is valued. The "Thin Green Line" movement demonstrates that "the most important factor, though, was the sheer number of people that activists mobilized by reaching beyond their traditional alliances." "Community members attended public hearings," "marched in protests," and "spent nights and weekends at kitchen tables drafting pleas to government agencies." This reflects a cultural value on persistent, determined action for the common good.

- However, stubbornness that becomes inflexible or obstructive to necessary cooperation or progress would be inconsistent with Cascadian values. The bioregional movement emphasizes "interdependence, sustainability, and resiliency" and the "connections between the individual, the community and the bioregion which increase the ability of each to support itself." This suggests that stubbornness must be balanced with community cooperation and shared goals.

- The ecology and society article describes "property rights, environmental ethics, rules of earning and holding titles, public accountability, and the reciprocal exchange system" as characteristics of the potlatch system among Northwest Coast peoples. "The leadership position of titleholders was contingent upon good stewardship of the land and upon the ability to give away a surplus generated from the land." "Failure to care for the land under his control was evidence that a titleholder was not worthy of his position." This suggests that persistence in stewardship is valued, but only when combined with responsibility to others.
