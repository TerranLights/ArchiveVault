## Findings
- Security in Cascadia is shaped by the region's geological reality. The Cascadia Subduction Zone, a 700-mile fault running from Northern California to British Columbia, poses a significant risk of a 9.0+ magnitude earthquake and a resulting tsunami up to 100 feet in height. This constant awareness of geological risk shapes the region's security culture.

- The Pacific Northwest Seismic Network emphasizes that "living safely here requires us to recognize the geologic hazards of our region and to take steps to protect ourselves, our communities, and our businesses from the potential losses these hazards can create." Security practices include signing up for emergency alerts, developing emergency plans, building emergency kits with a two-week supply of basic necessities, and protecting homes from earthquake hazards.

- The "Be 2 Weeks Ready" campaign, promoted by the Oregon Department of Emergency Management, reflects a cultural value on self-sufficiency and community preparedness. The campaign emphasizes "individuals, families, schools, government and communities can take steps to prepare" and that "the best thing you need to do is stay in your own neighborhood" after a major disaster.

- Community resilience is a key aspect of Cascadian security. The Kurihara article on winter readiness notes that "there are unique hazards in the Pacific Northwest due to our geography, so we must have a sense of community responsibility." Practical steps include joining CERT (Community Emergency Response Team), using amateur radio, and having an emergency go-bag.

- The Cascadia CoPes Hub works with "communities in the Pacific Northwest, including Washington, Oregon, and Northern California to increase their ability to mitigate and adapt to impacts from hazards like earthquakes, tsunamis, sea level rise, landslides, erosion, and climate change." This reflects a broader cultural commitment to resilience and adaptation.

- Security in Cascadia is also practiced through preparation for natural disasters, but also through the maintenance of critical infrastructure. The Oregon Resilience Plan emphasizes "investing in hazard mitigation" and "strengthening critical facilities" to ensure they are "available when communities need them most."

- The cultural value of independence influences how security is practiced. The bioregional Congress of Pacific Cascadia 1988 promotes "individual, community, and regional self-reliance and autonomy, wherever possible, in meeting survival and life-enhancing needs." This suggests that Cascadians value the ability to care for themselves and their communities in the face of potential threats.

- Security that is undermined by victimhood or passivity is inconsistent with Cascadian values. The Cascadia PoCesHub article notes that "being prepared for the Big One is not about predicting the future, but is about understanding the risk we already know about and taking the necessary steps to make communities more resilient when the next major disaster event occurs." This suggests that a proactive, preparedness-oriented culture is prized over a reactive or fatalistic approach.

- Security that fails to protect the community's most vulnerable members is also a concern. The Pacific Northwest Seismic Network emphasizes "it is important to consider and include people who are elderly or disabled in your plan" and "be sure to include special supplies for children, elderly people, disabled people, and pets in your kit." This reflects a cultural value on protecting all community members.
