## Findings
- Self-worth in Cascadia is shaped by a blend of individualism and community contribution. The region's cultural identity is rooted in "fierce individualism" and the "go-it-alone," "do-it-yourself" pioneers, loggers, and fur traders who settled the region. Cascadians tend to value self-reliance, independence, and authenticity as markers of personal worth.

- The concept of "authentic self" is central to Cascadian self-worth. The Comment Magazine article notes that "the first priority for many of these spiritualties is the accessing and the realizing one's 'authentic self.'" Cascadians tend to see themselves as "liberated personalities, freethinking in their politics, sexuality, technology, religion, and artistic creativity." The region's culture emphasizes "do-it-yourself" optimism and "you do you" orientation.

- However, Cascadian self-worth is also shaped by community and egalitarianism. The Seattle Times article on "Finding Community" notes that "we value personal autonomy, we also recognize our interconnectedness within a democratic society" and that "the social fabric that once kept individual ambition in check has weakened, leading to a culture of competition and materialism." This suggests that Cascadians are aware of the tension between individualism and community responsibility.

- The region's history of cooperative and communal businesses and organizations, including Group Health, REI, MEC, Puget Consumers Co-op, and numerous granges and mutual aid societies, reflects a cultural value that individual success is tied to community support. The Pacific Northwest has also "a long history of publicly owned power and utilities, with many of the region's cities owning their own public utilities."

- Self-worth in Cascadia is also shaped by moral capital and hard work. The WSU Magazine article on "Outside In" describes how "outwardly exhibiting one's moral worth can result in opportunities that are denied to those who appear to lack it" and that "morality was both a positive force, helping people to access social and economic capital, and a negative control that helped discourage behaviors that were seen as damaging to the community and its families." In the context of Golden Valley, "morality manifests most clearly not with regard to religious doctrines and beliefs, but more commonly in the sense of uniquely American frontier values such as independence, self-sufficiency, hard work, and 'family values.'"

- The region's low institutional religiosity and "secular-but-spiritual" orientation shape self-worth through "reverence for the Earth" and "nature reverence." The BC Studies article on "Religion at the Edge" describes "Reverential Naturalism" as "the essential foundation of regional spirituality," inspired by "Indigenous tradition and the modern environmental movement."

- Self-worth in Cascadia is also tied to contribution to the community. The Seattle Times article on "When our PNW community pulls together" notes that "our region is defined by a can-do spirit, grounded in innovation, adventure and collaboration" and that "the heart of who we are and what is possible when we combine our efforts." This suggests that Cascadians value both individual achievement and community contribution.

- Damage to self-worth in Cascadia can occur when an individual is seen as not contributing, not being self-reliant, or failing to maintain "authentic" identity. The Comment Magazine article notes that "families, civic organizations, and religious communities all suffer from the abiding desire for personal autonomy," suggesting that a lack of balance between individualism and community can damage self-worth.
