## Findings
- Possession in Cascadia is practiced through a dual emphasis on individual ownership and communal stewardship. The region's cultural narrative, rooted in bioregionalism, treats land, water, and natural resources as "the common heritage of all life," and this is reflected in the practice of trusteeship and stewardship. The bioregional Congress of Pacific Cascadia 1988 explicitly frames possession and ownership around ecological sustainability, self-reliance, and responsibility to the whole web of life.

- Cascadians tend to view ownership not merely as a legal right but as a relationship with the land that carries duties. The Cascadia Prairie-Oak Partnership's investment strategy highlights "ecocultural stewardship, including Indigenous knowledge, into conservation practice" and "tribal access to ancestral homelands and culturally important resources through ownership and management agreements." This reflects a cultural shift from raw possession to managed stewardship, where ownership is understood as a trust rather than a claim.

- The region's identity is tied to the land: salmon rivers, rugged Pacific coastline, and remnant stands of temperate rainforest. Ownership of these resources is a key aspect of Cascadian identity and independence. The bioregional movement emphasizes "local operators to be responsible for all aspects of the work process" and "stable communities with viable and permanent forest-based economics."

- Possession is also practiced through cooperative and community-based economic forms. The bioregional Congress explicitly promotes "cooperative, worker-owned, democratic forms of economic activity" and "an economy which should be self-regulating and self-regenerating." This suggests that possessive practices in Cascadia are channeled through community and cooperative structures rather than purely individual accumulation.

- The cultural value of independence reinforces the practice of possession: Cascadians "pride in the natural land and a sense of independence" across all Cascadian movements. This independence is grounded in a "shared history as a unified Oregon Territory" and is expressed through regional ownership of resources and self-determination.

- Possession that is purely hoarding or exclusionary is inconsistent with the bioregionalist ethos. The movement is based on a "shift to an ecocentric worldview" in which "political boundaries should match ecological and cultural boundaries" and communities are "nested within layers of relationships." This suggests that possessive behavior that harms the ecological community is socially disapproved of.
