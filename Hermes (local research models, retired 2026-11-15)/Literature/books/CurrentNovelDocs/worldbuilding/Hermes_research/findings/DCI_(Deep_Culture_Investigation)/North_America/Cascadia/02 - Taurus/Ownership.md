## Findings
- Ownership in Cascadia is understood as a legal and social right that carries responsibilities. The region's environmental values emphasize that ownership is a trust: land, water, and natural resources are held not just for individuals but for future generations and the broader community. The bioregional Congress of Pacific Cascadia 1988 frames ownership around "trusteeship and stewardship" and "ecological sustainability."

- Land ownership in Cascadia is marked by the presence of Indigenous sovereignty. Tribal nations hold treaty-reserved rights, and "co-stewardship agreements" between tribes and federal agencies have become increasingly common. The Boldt Decision (United States v. Washington, 1974) allocated 50% of the harvestable salmon catch to treaty Tribes, creating ongoing joint fisheries management structures. This reflects a cultural recognition that ownership involves shared authority and treaty obligations.

- The Cascadia Wildlands organization's approach to public lands management emphasizes "active and meaningful consultation with sovereign Indigenous Nations" and "incorporation of Indigenous knowledge" as "an essential part of sound management of public forests." This reflects a cultural norm where ownership of public lands is accountable to community input and Indigenous knowledge.

- Ownership in Cascadia is practiced through conservation and stewardship. The Cascadia Conservation District's programs show how landowners practice "stewardship efforts to increase diversity, resiliency, habitat, food & products" through "forest thinning, native & diverse plantings, soil building, intentional habitat growth and nourishing the water basin through slowing, spreading and sinking water flows." This suggests that ownership is expressed through active care rather than passive claim.

- The Prairie Oaks investment strategy highlights "fee-title acquisitions of lands and conservation easements" as tools for "protection and rebuild[ing] healthy oak and prairie systems across the Pacific Northwest." Ownership is thus also practiced through conservation mechanisms that limit extraction and preserve ecological function.

- The Northwest Forest Plan Amendment emphasizes that "cultural practices and stewardship (e.g., burning, tending, tracking, cultivating, and paying attention to aquatic species and wildlife habits and interactions, often through sacred ceremonies) have contributed to the establishment and maintenance of mature and old growth forest habitats." This reflects a cultural understanding of ownership as cultural and spiritual as well as legal.

- Ownership of natural resources in Cascadia is also practiced through cooperative and community-based forms. The bioregional Congress promotes "cooperative, worker-owned, democratic forms of economic activity" and "an economy which should be self-regulating and self-regenerating." This suggests that possessive practices in Cascadia are channeled through community and cooperative structures rather than purely individual accumulation.

- Ownership that is purely exclusionary is inconsistent with Cascadian values. The bioregional movement emphasizes "responsibility to the well-being of the region" and "self-reliance, economic democracy, and responsibility to the whole web of life." This suggests that ownership rights are balanced against community and ecological responsibilities.
