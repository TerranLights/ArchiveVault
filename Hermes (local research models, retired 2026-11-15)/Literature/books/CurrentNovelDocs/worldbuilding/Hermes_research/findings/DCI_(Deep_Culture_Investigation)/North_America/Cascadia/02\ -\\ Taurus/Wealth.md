## Findings
- Wealth in Cascadia is accumulated and displayed through a combination of technology, entrepreneurship, and resource-based industries. The region's economy, with its "strongest economic corridor" stretching from Vancouver through Seattle to Portland and Eugene, is anchored by technology (Microsoft, Amazon, Boeing, Apple-adjacent firms) and resource sectors (forestry, fishing, agriculture).

- The Cascadia Movement describes the region as "home to some of the world's wealthiest corporations while many neighbors struggle with poverty," reflecting a cultural awareness of wealth inequality. The movement also notes that "it leads in green technology, energy, and localized systems of resilience and community," suggesting that wealth is expected to contribute to regional resilience and community benefit.

- The entrepreneurial spirit is a core expression of Cascadian wealth. The Christ & Cascadia article notes that "none is more pronounced than its entrepreneurial spirit" and lists entrepreneurs including Frederick Weyerhaeuser (wood products, sustainable forestry), Bill Boeing (aircraft), John Nordstrom (retail), Bill Gates (Microsoft), Jeff Brotman (Costco), Howard Schultz (Starbucks), and Jeff Bezos (Amazon). This reflects a cultural value on individual enterprise and innovation as paths to wealth.

- The First Nation Partnership — Cascadia Department of Bioregion provides detailed economic data: "Cascadia would be home to slightly more than 16 million people" with an economy generating "more than $1.112437 trillion in goods and services annually." The most dominant sectors are "agriculture, science and technology, trade, media, and tourism." "Cascadia is home to the second largest tech hub in the world, one of the highest standards of living, and advanced medical and educational institutions."

- Wealth in Cascadia is also tied to the region's natural resource base. The NWCouncil report notes that "the Pacific Northwest's natural resources — rivers, forests, minerals, water, and fish and wildlife — provide raw materials and energy for economic development." The region is "the Nation's top producer of 28 different agricultural products" and "one of the leading producers of timber products." The NOAA report notes that "the agriculture, forestry, and fisheries sectors accounted for over 700,000 jobs and almost $140 billion in sales."

- The US Forest Service highlights "growing economic opportunities" in sectors like "mass timber" and water-based industries. "Waterflows from the National Forest System support millions of municipal, industrial, and agricultural users." "A thriving mass timber market can help us reduce hazardous fuels, protect homes and communities from fire risk, and boost rural jobs and economies."

- The Nature Conservancy highlights "investing in nature pays off" through "sustainable shellfish and seaweed projects that create jobs, strengthen local seafood supply and benefit Tribal nations and coastal communities." "Salmon sustain food systems, ways of life and the economy of the Pacific Northwest." "The Makah Tribe is building a new local economy rooted in sustainable forestry."

- Wealth is expected to be shared within the community. The Cascadia Movement states that "every community affected must have a substantial role in the decision making process" and "the people living here, rather than in power centers thousands of miles away, are best positioned to determine the right course of action for themselves and their communities." This suggests that wealth accumulation is expected to be connected to community benefit.

- Wealth that is hoarded or extracted without community benefit is inconsistent with Cascadian values. The bioregional movement emphasizes "self-reliance, sustainability, networking and locally based disaster preparedness" and "connections between the individual, the community and the bioregion which increase the ability of each to support itself." The "Salmon Nation" network "want to build a new resilient economic and social base" with "business models across Cascadia that are sustainable and profitable – including small, locally owned businesses."

- Wealth display in Cascadia is influenced by cultural values of humility and community contribution. The Seattle Times article on "Finding Community" notes that "we value personal autonomy, we also recognize our interconnectedness within a democratic society" and that "the social fabric that once kept individual ambition in check has weakened, leading to a culture of competition and materialism." This suggests that excessive wealth display without community contribution would be socially disapproved of.
