## Findings
- Possessiveness in Cascadia is expressed differently at different levels. At the community level, protective possessiveness over one's family, home, and local community is socially accepted and even valued. The NATIVE TRANSFORMATIONS study describes "protective communities" that "build interdependence and provide emotional support" and engage in "traditional subsistence practices, including fishing, hunting, and gathering, with the sharing of resources among their members." This suggests that possessive behavior directed toward protecting community resources is valued.

- At the same time, excessive possessiveness that manifests as hoarding, exclusionary behavior, or refusal to share resources with the community is generally viewed negatively. The Seattle Times article on "Finding Community" notes that "community is fundamentally about the collective welfare; after all, if we are not in this together, we are isolated." The article also emphasizes that "residents have consistently invested in public goods—libraries, recreational facilities, housing for the elderly—often through self-imposed taxes," indicating a cultural value on shared resource management.

- The potlatch system of the Northwest Coast provides a historical model of possessiveness balanced with generosity. The ecology and society article describes "property rights, environmental ethics, rules of earning and holding titles, public accountability, and the reciprocal exchange system" as characteristics of the potlatch system. "The titleholders of houses were the proprietors of the lands of the house, with the ability to exclude others, manage, harvest, and bequest the lands and resources." However, "the head titleholder was in charge of land management; no one could use the land without permission" and "trespass was a capital offense." This reflects a cultural understanding of possessiveness as a responsibility: "the leadership position of titleholders was contingent upon good stewardship of the land and upon the ability to give away a surplus generated from the land." "The requirement that titleholders be good stewards of the resources under their control was characteristic of the ethical systems of the region." "Failure to care for the land under his control was evidence that a titleholder was not worthy of his position."

- Community Land Trusts (CLTs) in Cascadia reflect a modern expression of possessiveness balanced with community access. The "Crossing borders" article describes "collecKve land tenure and equitable access, developed primarily by Community Land Trusts (CLTs) to counteract land speculaKon, stabilise community land pracKces, and expand long-term access to land and housing." This suggests that possessive behavior in the form of land ownership is expected to serve community purposes.

- The Cascadia Movement emphasizes "community resilience" and "bioregional planning" with a focus on protecting "the bioregion's unique ecosystems." Groups like "Cascadia Earth First, the Cascadia Forest Defenders, and Cascadia Wildlands formed to help protect the bioregion's unique ecosystems," reflecting a possessive devotion to the land and environment.

- The Cascadia Conservation District's Strategic Plan 2025-2030 emphasizes "wise stewardship and conservation through best management practices for all natural resources for current and future residents." This reflects a cultural value on stewardship that extends possession beyond individual ownership to include responsibility for future residents.

- The National Observer article on "Fighting climate change by reconnecting to ecosystems in our own backyards" notes that "community knowledge of their own ecosystems, no one is better equipped to protect the bioregion than those who call it home," said Brandon Letsinger. This reflects a possessive devotion to one's home bioregion and community.

- Possessiveness that blocks necessary cooperation or shared resource management would be inconsistent with Cascadian values. The bioregional movement emphasizes "interdependence, sustainability, and resiliency for the Cascadia bioregion, starting from our watersheds." The movement's focus on "regenerate[ing] our bioregions" and "cultivate the conditions for place-based movements, cultures, and ways of living in place to thrive" suggests that possessive behavior must be balanced with sharing and cooperation.

- In Cascadia, possessiveness is also expressed through environmental and cultural stewardship. The "Crossing borders" article notes that "ecological and cultural landscape stewardship, understood as a broad set of practices aimed at maintaining, protecting, and enhancing landscapes shaped by ecological processes and long-standing cultural relations, including traditional land-use practices and Indigenous forms of territorial care." This suggests that possessiveness over land and resources is expected to be exercised with care and responsibility.
